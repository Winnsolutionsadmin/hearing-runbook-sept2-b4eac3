STEP 1 · Your opening — about two minutes, then the video

This is the only part you deliver uninterrupted. It's a story with one legal sentence at the front. Exhibit 2 appears once (the receipt) and Exhibit 3 is the video you play. Nothing else gets a number here.

OPEN“Good afternoon, Your Honor. Jarred Winn. I know you've read my exhibits, so I'll keep this short.
THE ONE LEGAL SENTENCE — SAY IT FIRST, BECAUSE THEIR LETTER LEADS WITH “NON-REFUNDABLE”I want to say one thing up front, because their letter to me starts with it: this isn't a cancellation. I never tried to cancel. We showed up, I paid, we stayed. A non-refundable rate covers a guest who changes the booking, cancels it, or doesn't show up — a guest who chooses not to use the room. I did none of those. I used the room, and they didn't deliver the night. That's what this is about.
WHAT HAPPENED — YOUR WORDS, YOUR FAMILYOn December 28th I booked one night at One Village Place at Northstar for my wife and our three kids — a two-bedroom, about $2,900 through Hotels.com, booked that same afternoon. That's the receipt, Exhibit 2. It was my birthday. The whole point of the night was simple: get the kids to sleep, get up, and spend my birthday snowboarding with them.

At three in the morning, snow-removal machinery and backup alarms started right outside our room, and they ran until about five. I got up and called the front desk. I was told there was nothing they could do. So I lay there with my wife and three kids awake in the dark, listening to backup alarms for two hours. I recorded a piece of it at about 4:15, through a closed window, so you could hear what we heard. It understates it. That's Exhibit 3 — I'd like to play it.”
PLAY EXHIBIT 3 — once, about 25 seconds, say nothing while it playsScreen-share the local file (share only the video window). If the judge prefers, ask him to open Exhibit 3 from the evidence system.
AFTER THE VIDEO — THE PART THAT MAKES IT THEIR FAULT“Nobody slept. In the morning the kids were wrecked and we never made it onto the mountain. The one day we'd paid $2,900 for — my birthday with my kids — didn't happen.

Here's the thing, Your Honor. This wasn't a surprise to them. When I complained, they didn't call it an emergency — their own email told me snow removal ‘always happens early in the morning.’ That's a schedule. They had it that afternoon when they took my money. I didn't. And in their January letter, when they offered me points to come back some other time, they wrote — and this is a quote — that if I came back, ‘similar winter-related conditions, including snow removal and operational sounds, may still be present.’ So they know exactly how to warn a family. They did it for a free night they were offering. For the night I actually paid for, they said nothing. No word at booking, nothing at check-in, no offer to move us, nothing when I called at three in the morning.

I gave them three months to make it right. I told them I'd take a quiet replacement night instead of any money. They never answered that — every reply was the same points offer. I'm here because I ran out of other doors.”

Then stop. Don't fill silence. The judge will either ask you questions or turn to Mr. Kocarek. (You have NOT mentioned the earplug remark on purpose. It's your most attackable fact; it comes out when it's earned — Step 3.)

STEP 2 · The judge asks you questions

Short, true, human. Each card: the question, then what to say. Where an exhibit belongs, it's named in the answer.

“Did you call the front desk during the night?”
“Yes. From the room phone, sometime after three. I was told there was nothing they could do. In the morning I went down to the desk and asked for the manager — there wasn't one available for five days.”
If asked who / for a record: “I don't remember a name — it was the middle of the night. It was the room phone; their switchboard would have it, I don't.” Say only what you're sure of. Don't volunteer doubt.
“Did you ask to move rooms or check out?”
“I called and was told nothing could be done. It was three in the morning, three kids, a snowed-in mountain town at the busiest week of the year. There was nowhere to take five people.”
“How long is the video? Was it just that moment?”
“It's about half a minute at 4:15. I wasn't going to stand there filming for two hours — I was trying to get my kids back to sleep. It ran from about three to five.”
“You stayed the whole night. You used the room.”
“We did — we had nowhere else to be. But a room where a family can't sleep is worse than no room. What I paid for was a night's rest before one day on the mountain, and that's the part that was taken away.”
“Your claim form says $4,500. Your letters said $3,000, then $3,200. Why?”
“When I filed I put down an estimate that included our time and the ruined day. I understand the Court can't award that, so I've brought it down to exactly what's on the receipt, Exhibit 2. The earlier numbers were settlement letters.”
“Who paid — you or a company?”
“My card. I paid, my family stayed, the receipt is in my name.” Stop there.
“Did you get anything back from Hotels.com?”
“Only a $300 goodwill credit they gave me on their own — I put that in my hearing statement, Exhibit 0. The hotel refunded nothing.”
“Is there anything you'd like me to ask Mr. Kocarek?”
Only if he has disputed the earplug remark: “Whether the property keeps earplugs in the gondola-side rooms, Your Honor.” Otherwise: “No, Your Honor — I'll rest on the exhibits.”

STEP 3 · Kocarek's turn, then your reply — this is where the exhibits live

He'll speak for a few minutes. Take notes; don't interrupt. Then the judge will let you respond. Reply only to what he actually said — each card below is “when you hear this → say this.” The exhibit number goes in the sentence; that's how you “use” an exhibit in small claims.

WHEN HE SAYS: “He booked through Expedia / Hotels.com — his refund is their problem, not ours.”
“I did exactly what they suggested. Hotels.com went to the hotel and asked for the refund on my behalf, and the hotel said no to them too — that's Exhibit 7. The hotel is the one that checked us in, gave us the room, charged me $44 at the desk, and answered my complaint for four months. Only they could release the money, and they wouldn't.”
WHEN HE SAYS: “We don't control the snow removal. That's the Village / the mountain.”
“Their January letter, Exhibit 5, says they don't control the gondola. The snow removal, they defend in the same letter as ‘necessary for guest safety.’ And Your Honor — I'm not asking them to control the plow. They control whether they sell that room, same day, without a word. They control whether they help a family that calls at three in the morning. And their own website, Exhibit 12, from twenty days before I booked, posts a notice about a renovation and one about a broken hot tub — so they know how to warn guests when something at the property matters. They just didn't warn us about this.”
Never say “gondola” when talking about Exhibit 12. Never say it promised quiet.
WHEN HE SAYS: “It was a historic storm. Safety work had to happen.”
“They didn't call it an emergency when I complained — they told me it ‘always happens early in the morning,’ Exhibit 6. That's a schedule, not an emergency. And it wasn't snowing that night — the storm had ended two days before; that's the weather record, Exhibit 9. I'm not saying the work shouldn't have been done. I'm saying nothing stopped them from telling us, or giving us a different room, or offering to move us.”
WHEN HE SAYS: “We offered 50,000 — then 60,000 — points. That's more than the night was worth.”
“I never accepted them and never signed anything. And it wasn't what I asked for — I asked for a quiet replacement night instead of any refund, and nobody ever answered that. Points aren't a refund of the money I paid.”
One sentence, then move on. Never count the offers. Never say the offers prove they were wrong.
WHEN HE SAYS: “The rate was non-refundable. He agreed to it at booking.”
“I agreed I wouldn't get my money back if I changed the booking, cancelled it, or didn't show up. I did none of those. I checked in, I stayed, I checked out. That term is about a guest choosing not to use the room. It says nothing about what the hotel keeps when the hotel doesn't deliver the night it sold.”
Also true and worth knowing: the only place “non-refundable” appears anywhere in the evidence is THEIR January letter (Exhibit 5) saying so. The Hotels.com itinerary (Exhibit 2) doesn't state the policy. Don't lead with that — but if he waves the term around, you can say: “The rate terms aren't in evidence, Your Honor — the only thing in the record is their letter asserting it.”
IF HE PUSHES: “Non-refundable means no refund for any reason. Cancellations, no-shows, complaints — that's what he bought.”
“Then it's a rate that says the hotel gets paid even if it delivers nothing — and I don't think that's what anyone means by non-refundable. Every non-refundable rate I've ever seen is about my decision: change, cancel, no-show. Not one of them says ‘and if we run machinery outside your window all night, you still pay.’ If that's the deal they're selling, it needed to say so, and they needed to say so when they took my money that afternoon.”
Back pocket only if the judge wants law: a term written by one side is read against the side that wrote it (Civ. Code 1654); a clause that excuses the seller's own failure to perform is a forfeiture the law doesn't enforce (1668, 1671). Say the plain version; say the statutes only if asked for authority.
WHEN HE SAYS: “Nobody promised him silence. A bad night isn't a breach.”
“A $2,900 villa doesn't need the word ‘quiet’ in the listing to promise a night you can sleep through. And they knew what was scheduled outside that window and said nothing.”
WHEN HE SAYS: “This earplug story — an unnamed employee, no witness, brought up later.”
“I wrote it down to their Director of Operations in January — it's in my email in Exhibit 6. They answered that letter twice and never once said it wasn't said. And their own letter, Exhibit 5, admits the snowplow activity ‘impacted your experience.’ They've never denied any of this happened.”
This is the ONLY time the earplug remark comes up — when he raises it, or if the judge asks. Then it's earned.
WHEN HE SAYS: “He knew it was a snow week. He chose a room by the lift.”
“Knowing there's snow in the mountains isn't being told machinery will run outside your kids' window from three to five. They had that schedule. I didn't. One sentence when they took my booking and none of us are here.”
WHEN HE SAYS: “Half of that $2,881 wasn't even money — it was promotional credit.”
“The OneKeyCash was earned with money I'd already spent with them. It was taken out of my account and it was never put back. The hotel was paid the full $2,881 for the room either way. My loss is what I paid — not what the hotel netted after Expedia's commission.”
Ideally you've already said this in Step 4 before he gets to it. Never say “collateral source.”
WHEN HE SAYS: “He threatened us — chargebacks, regulators, ‘further escalation.’”
“I never threatened anyone. I wrote one sentence: please respond within three business days, or I'll take it to Expedia, my card company, or a consumer agency. That's in Exhibit 6. And that's exactly what I did, after three real letters went unanswered. Telling a company what happens next when they don't answer isn't a threat — it's a deadline.”
That is the ONLY sentence in four months of email that could be called a “threat” — your Jan 21 email: “Please respond within 3 business days to avoid further escalation, such as through Expedia, credit card disputes, or regulatory complaints.” Know it verbatim so he can't paraphrase it into something worse.
WHEN HE SAYS: “He claimed 95 decibels. He cited a county noise ordinance. He said ‘habitability’ and ‘quiet enjoyment’ — those are tenant laws.”
“I'm not a lawyer. In January I was a frustrated dad using the words I had. The decibel number was shorthand for ‘loud enough to keep a family up’ — I never claimed a calibrated measurement, and the Court can hear the video itself. What I meant then is what I'm saying now, in plain words: I paid for a night, and they didn't deliver it.”
WHEN HE SAYS: “He didn't show up in July.”
“The Court found that was inadvertence and reinstated the case, and Mr. Kocarek had no objection. I'd rather use the Court's time on what happened that night.” Zero defensiveness. Stop.
WHEN HE SAYS: “HV Global isn't the operator. Wrong entity.”
“Their officers handled this dispute with me for four months and Mr. Kocarek is here today for them — that's Exhibit 10 and Exhibit 6. If the exact operating name is different, I'd ask the Court to correct the name under section 116.560. There's no surprise here.”
If the judge wants to continue the case to fix the name, say yes. Never fight that.
IF THE JUDGE ASKS: “How many times did you write to them?”
“Twelve times over four months, Your Honor. Exhibit 6A lays it out on one page — every reply was the same points offer.”

STEP 4 · The money — when the judge asks what you want, or before he names a number

Two exhibits: Exhibit 2 (the receipt) and Exhibit 8 (costs). Say the OneKeyCash paragraph before anyone floats a partial figure — the most common “fair” number a judge reaches for is your cash-only $1,445, and that sentence is what stops it.

THE ASK“I'm asking for $2,881.17 — that's the exact price of the night on the receipt, Exhibit 2: $2,591.10 for the room and $290.07 in taxes. Plus the $44.48 in fees I paid at the property. And my costs — the $78 filing fee and $88 for service, that's Exhibit 8.”
HOW I PAID — SAY IT NOW, DON'T WAIT TO BE ASKED“The receipt shows how I paid: $1,445.05 on my card and $1,436.12 in OneKeyCash. Those rewards were earned with money I'd already spent with them, they were taken out of my account, and they were never given back. The hotel was paid the full $2,881 for the room either way.”
THE $300 — GIVE IT UP GRACEFULLY, ONCE“And for completeness — Hotels.com, not the hotel, gave me $300 in credit on their own. I put that in my statement. If the Court thinks it should come off the award, take it off. I'm asking for what the hotel kept.”
ONLY IF THE JUDGE FLOATS A PARTIAL NUMBER“Your Honor, what I bought was one night's sleep before a day on the mountain with my kids. We got neither. I'm asking for what that night cost — not a dollar more. The only price anyone has ever put on that night is theirs: $2,881. If they think the room still had some value that night, they've had eight months and three hearings to say what it was, and they never have. If the Court sees it differently, I'll respect that.”
Never name a partial number yourself. Never agree that $1,445 is “what you really paid.”
INTEREST — LAST, ONE SENTENCE“I'd also ask for interest from the day they refused in writing, January 13th — at the statutory rate that's about $183.”

STEP 5 · Closing — if the judge gives you a last word

SAY THIS AND SIT BACK“I gave them three months and every chance to fix this. I even said I'd take a quiet night some other time instead of any money. Every answer was the same points offer. I'm not here because I wanted to be — I'm here because that was the last door. I'm asking for the price of the night, the $44 in fees, and my costs. Thank you.”

STEP 6 · The ruling, and what to do in the ten minutes after

Judgment for you — any amount

“Thank you, Your Honor.” Nothing else. The Notice of Entry of Judgment (SC-130) comes by mail; they have 30 days from that to pay or appeal. If Kocarek offers cash right after — that is the only moment MVW ever has a reason to write a check (pay you now vs. send a lawyer to superior court): anything at or above the judgment less about 15% for finality is a yes. Points, never.

Taken under submission

“Thank you, Your Honor.” The decision comes by mail in a couple of weeks.

Judgment for the defendant

“Thank you, Your Honor.” No visible reaction. You can't appeal (CCP 116.710). Log off. We'll talk.

If he offers cash — before or after (the ladder)

  • $1,500 or more: take it. $1,200–1,500: take it if it's cash-final (check or refund to your card). Under $1,200: “I appreciate it, but I can't resolve it for that — I'd like to go ahead.”
  • His likely first number is $1,445 (“the cash you actually paid”). Counter once: “I could do $1,800, refunded to my card or by check.” If he holds at $1,445 cash-final, that's a yes.
  • Points, any amount: “I appreciate it, but I can only resolve this for cash.”
  • Put it on the record as a continuance, never a dismissal on a promise: “Your Honor, we've reached a settlement — $X, payable by [check / refund to card] within ten business days. I'd ask the Court to continue the matter to a date after that rather than dismiss it today.”
WORDS THAT COST YOU MONEY — DON'T SAY THEM

“distress” · “suffering” · “trauma” · “ordinance” · “Sunday” · a decibel number as a fact · “quiet enjoyment” · “habitability” · “collateral source” · “essential purpose” · “fraud” · “the points prove they knew they were wrong” · “Northstar Lodge” · “gondola” when you cue Exhibit 12 · anything about the 2015 TripAdvisor review (it isn't in evidence) · anything about how the video file was handled · “the work was unnecessary” · anything blaming the court about July.

You can say: my wife, my kids, awake in the dark, backup alarms, wrecked, the one day we'd paid for, frustrated dad, ran out of doors. That's not emotional — that's what happened.
